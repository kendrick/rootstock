# Heat-Limited Products in the Seeded Rules

Researched 2026-09-29 against the Rules in `src/seed/rules.json`.

## Answer

No seeded Rule's product carries a high-temperature restriction, either on its label or in the extension sources behind it. On the evidence, no Rule should get a `heat-limited` tag, and no source gives a number for a Guard to use. The only herbicide in the seed is prodiamine (Barricade 4FL), a pre-emergent whose label has no air-temperature ceiling. Its heat language covers spray droplet size in "hot and dry conditions" and injury to turf "stressed by conditions such as drought, low fertility, or pest damage." None of the three fertilizer Rules names a product label. For the lawn, AgriLife advises against fertilizing bermudagrass "under heat or drought stress" and "during hot, dry periods in the late summer." That's a turf-condition limit with no temperature attached. The 90°F figure comes from post-emergent broadleaf herbicides, especially 2,4-D esters (volatility) and three-way turf products (turf injury). For bermudagrass the post-emergent guidance is stricter still, at 85°F. If the owner adds a post-emergent Rule, that Rule should get `heat-limited`, with the limit taken from its own label, 85°F for a SpeedZone Southern-type product. If the owner wants to keep the 90°F practice on the current Rules anyway, the Guard's source should be `kind: "owner"`, not extension or label. `last-nitrogen` is the only current Rule an extension source even arguably backs, and its basis is heat and drought stress on the turf, not a forecast high.

The "don't fertilize in summer" advice in the lawn sources concerns timing and turf condition, and none of it names a temperature. The FAQ allows summer nitrogen outright: "Supplemental N applications (if desired) are spaced at least 45 to 60 days apart. Newly established or previously neglected lawns can benefit from summer applications. Slow release N sources are particularly suited for summer fertilizer applications." Its burn warning is tied to liquid N above a rate, and it tells the reader to apply to a dry lawn and water in, with no temperature attached. The calendar's "hot, dry periods ... when moisture may be limiting" describes drought stress on the turf, which a soil-moisture or rainfall check would catch better than a forecast high.

## Post-Emergent Herbicides in the Seed

There are none. The three herbicide Rules all cite the Barricade 4FL label, and Barricade is labeled "For selective preemergence control of grass and broadleaf weeds." Its label lists post-emergent tank-mix partners (glyphosate, Finale and others) for ornamentals. If the owner ever tank-mixes one, the partner's label sets its own weather limits.

## Where the 90°F Convention Comes From

The number traces to post-emergent herbicide labels, which cap air temperature for volatility or for turf injury.

On volatility, 2,4-D ester formulations can vaporize in heat and drift onto nearby plants. An older low-volatile ester label states the number outright: "Do not apply when temperature exceeds 90°F." (600 LV 2,4-D Weed Killer, EPA Reg. No. 2217-413, EPA-stamped label dated 2002-03-08. The scanned text reads "gO°F", an OCR error for 90°F.) https://www3.epa.gov/pesticides/chem_search/ppls/002217-00413-20020308.pdf. Current ester labels dropped the number for a condition: "2,4-D esters may volatilize during conditions of low humidity and high temperatures. Do not apply during conditions of low humidity and high temperatures." (2,4-D L.V. 4 Ester, EPA Reg. No. 228-139, 2023-02-21) https://www3.epa.gov/pesticides/chem_search/ppls/000228-00139-20230221.pdf. Dicamba follows the same volatility logic, with a forecast-based limit at a higher number. EPA's 2026 over-the-top dicamba labels for cotton and soybean say "No applications may occur if the temperature is forecasted to be at or above 95°F on the day of or the day after a planned application," and they cap the acreage treated between 85 and 95°F. https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-implements-strongest-protections-agency-history-over-top-dicamba-use-cotton-and (EPA news release, 2026-02-06, not the label text). That's an agricultural use, not a lawn one, but it shows how a forecast-high Guard maps onto label language.

On turf injury, three-way broadleaf products for warm-season lawns cap air temperature to protect the grass. SpeedZone Southern (2,4-D ester, mecoprop-p, dicamba and carfentrazone; EPA Reg. No. 2217-835) says: "Do not broadcast apply this product when ambient temperatures are below 50°F or above 85°F; some injury may be expected with spot treatments when air temperatures exceed 85°F." It adds: "To avoid turf injury, use only on turfgrass that is reasonably free of stress from diseases, insects, excess heat or cold, drought or excess rainfall/irrigation, shaded areas, low soil pH, nematodes, improper mowing or improper applications of fertilizer and pesticides." https://www.domyown.com/msds/SpeedZoneSouthern_label.pdf. This is a retailer-hosted copy of the manufacturer's specimen label, and I didn't pull the matching EPA PPLS copy. Clemson's HGIC lawn fact sheet (land-grant extension, updated 2022-07-13) gives both numbers. For August–October post-emergence work it says "Typically, spray when temperatures are below 90 °F to reduce injury to the lawn." Its herbicide table footnotes say "Intermediate safety on bermudagrass. Do not treat weeds in bermudagrass if temperatures are above 85 degrees." and "Spot treatments to St. Augustinegrass at temperatures above 90 degrees may cause temporary growth regulation." https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/managing-weeds-in-warm-season-lawns/

Fertilizer burn isn't a source of the number. No lawn, fig or container source I read attaches 90°F, or any air temperature, to fertilizing. The AgriLife lawn guidance runs on turf stress, product type and rate, as quoted in the table. AgriLife's bermudagrass calendar gives no upper limit for herbicides either. Its only herbicide temperature is a floor for perennial broadleaf post-emergents: "These herbicides will not be effective when daytime temperatures are less than 60 °F."
